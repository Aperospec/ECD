# Editorial Design Skill

## Professional role

Editorial Design turns an approved communication structure, exact copy, visual direction, and source material into a deliberate page or sequence system.

It is not decoration, template selection, image prompting, or final production. It is the professional act of organizing meaning through scale, typography, image, space, rhythm, crop, alignment, contrast, and sequence.

The Skill reports to Art Director. It does not approve its own formal result and does not report directly to Creative Producer or the Executive Creative Director.

## Required inputs

Before formal design begins, require:

- Greenlit Creative Treatment;
- ECD-aligned Creative Script;
- exact page and companion copy;
- Frame Script and Written Visual Requirements;
- visual problem and current visual thesis;
- target surface, geometry, viewing condition, safe areas, and variants;
- source assets, evidence, references, rights, and limitations;
- current locks and open visual variables;
- Art Director assignment and acceptance criteria.

If exact copy, page purpose, evidence, or target geometry is unresolved, return the issue to Art Director rather than inventing an answer inside layout.

## Core design model

Every page is shaped by three interacting systems:

### 1. Editorial forces

- what must be understood first;
- what must be understood later;
- what can be implied visually;
- what must be read exactly;
- what evidence or qualification must remain inspectable;
- what emotional or cognitive change the page must produce.

### 2. Visual forces

- dominant mass;
- counterweight;
- tension and release;
- direction of movement;
- figure / ground relationship;
- scale contrast;
- density contrast;
- image / type power balance;
- depth and overlap;
- continuity with adjacent pages.

### 3. Surface constraints

- aspect ratio;
- feed thumbnail;
- actual mobile width;
- platform overlays and crop;
- language and glyph behavior;
- source-asset resolution;
- evidence legibility;
- production and variant requirements.

Professional design resolves these systems together. It does not finish one and attach the others afterward.

## Method

### Stage 1 — Reconstruct the design problem

Translate the Art Director assignment into a page-design problem.

Record:

```markdown
Page / sequence role:
Audience state before viewing:
Audience state after viewing:
Primary message:
Secondary support:
Exact copy tiers:
Required visual event or evidence:
Target surface and viewing distance:
Visual thesis inherited from Art Director:
Main risk of misreading:
Main risk of generic design:
```

Do not begin from style words such as `cinematic`, `premium`, `futuristic`, or `editorial`. Begin from the relationship the page must make perceptible.

### Stage 2 — Build a content hierarchy map

Classify every content unit by function, not by source order.

Typical tiers:

- dominant statement or identity;
- explanatory deck;
- supporting argument;
- evidence or object label;
- source, limitation, date, version, or attribution;
- navigational or sequence marker;
- optional editorial texture.

For each unit, define:

```markdown
Meaning priority:
Reading priority:
Required exactness:
Required visibility at thumbnail / mobile / full size:
Relationship to image or evidence:
Can be omitted: [yes / no]
Can be compressed: [within which authority]
```

Source documents often present content in writing order. Design order must follow perceptual and editorial priority.

### Stage 3 — Choose the page strategy

Select a page strategy based on the communication problem, not an aesthetic preference.

Possible primary strategies include:

- type-led statement;
- image-led hero;
- image–type interlock;
- evidence-led explanation;
- comparison;
- process or system;
- modular editorial field;
- extreme reduction;
- cumulative sequence;
- spatial metaphor;
- annotated artifact;
- controlled collision.

State why the chosen strategy is more appropriate than at least one plausible alternative.

A strategy is not a template. It defines the hierarchy and relationship that the layout must solve.

### Stage 4 — Explore with thumbnails

Do not move directly from brief to one polished layout.

Create a set of materially different low-cost compositions. The number depends on novelty and risk; use enough to test genuinely different structures.

Vary consequential axes such as:

- type-dominant versus image-dominant;
- concentrated versus distributed mass;
- static versus directional composition;
- separated versus interlocked image and type;
- centered stability versus asymmetric tension;
- close crop versus environmental scale;
- single focal object versus modular evidence field;
- dense middle versus sparse field;
- continuous sequence versus page-by-page contrast.

Hold irrelevant styling details constant during structural exploration.

Each thumbnail must answer:

- What is the first visual mass?
- Where does the eye move next?
- What gives the composition tension or stability?
- Where can the exact copy actually live?
- What content remains protected?
- Why is this structure specific to the project?

Reject thumbnails that differ only in font, color, or small placement changes.

### Stage 5 — Select and justify a composition

Compare alternatives by consequence, not taste alone.

Evaluate:

- fidelity to page purpose;
- specificity to the content;
- first-glance legibility;
- ability to carry exact copy;
- image and evidence requirements;
- sequence role;
- target-surface behavior;
- production feasibility;
- potential for meaningful refinement.

Record the selected direction and why rejected alternatives were weaker. This prevents unconscious return to a rejected structure during later production.

### Stage 6 — Construct the grid and geometry

The grid is a system of relationships, not a visible decoration.

Choose geometry according to content:

- simple column systems for decisive statements;
- modular grids for mixed content and evidence;
- asymmetric grids for directional tension;
- baseline logic for text-rich pages;
- radial, diagonal, or custom geometry only when it clarifies the concept.

Define:

```markdown
Canvas and safe area:
Primary axes:
Columns / modules:
Margins and gutters:
Baseline or vertical rhythm:
Dominant mass zone:
Counterweight zone:
Image / evidence field:
Copy field:
Allowed grid violation:
Reason for violation:
```

Use the grid to create coherence. Break it only when the break produces a clear perceptual or conceptual effect.

### Stage 7 — Compose type as visual form

Typography is simultaneously language, rhythm, texture, scale, and geometry.

Work with the Typographic Design Skill to establish:

- type roles;
- font category and voice;
- dominant title shape;
- line-break logic;
- line length;
- leading and paragraph rhythm;
- alignment behavior;
- CJK / Latin / numeral relationship;
- punctuation treatment;
- hierarchy and contrast;
- minimum legible scale.

Begin with the largest typographic and image masses. Do not refine microcopy while the page's dominant geometry is unresolved.

A headline is not merely placed into a box. Its word shape, line endings, density, and negative shape must participate in the composition.

### Stage 8 — Resolve exact-copy geometry

Use the actual authoritative copy, not generic placeholder text, before committing any production-intent image.

For every page, establish:

- provisional or intended line breaks;
- copy footprint;
- hierarchy;
- reading order;
- primary and secondary text zones;
- minimum readable scale;
- variant behavior;
- protected image, face, product, interface, action, or evidence zones;
- permitted and forbidden overlap;
- local contrast requirements;
- crop and image-generation consequences.

This may be recorded in the Copy-Aware Layout Map, but the map is the result of design work, not a substitute for designing.

### Stage 9 — Integrate image and type

Select one governing image–type relationship per page or page class.

Possible relationships:

- separated fields with deliberate tension;
- text contained in negative space;
- controlled overlap across low-risk detail;
- subject occluding typography;
- typography crossing in front of subject;
- silhouette-responsive wrapping;
- typography framing or cropping the image;
- type-led composition with supporting imagery;
- evidence-led composition with typographic annotation.

Do not use every relationship at once.

When imagery must be created, give Image Direction explicit consequences from the selected layout:

- subject position, scale, orientation, and crop;
- required quiet or low-detail zones;
- protected zones;
- light / dark value behavior behind type;
- perspective and focal hierarchy;
- extension and variant latitude;
- layer and mask requirements.

An instruction such as `subject on the right, space on the left` is not sufficient when exact copy and visual interaction are consequential.

### Stage 10 — Design the sequence, not only pages

For multi-page work, view all pages simultaneously.

Establish:

- opening promise;
- sequence progression;
- density curve;
- scale curve;
- image / type alternation;
- repeated identifiers;
- deliberate page-class changes;
- evidence timing;
- climax, payoff, or aftertaste;
- relationship between final page and cover.

A coherent sequence is not ten versions of one page. Variation must correspond to changing editorial jobs.

Use a sequence contact sheet throughout development. Individual pages that look good alone may still damage the whole set through repetition, imbalance, or premature climax.

### Stage 11 — Refine optically

After structural decisions are stable, refine:

- visual center of gravity;
- perceived rather than mathematical alignment;
- edge tension;
- crop pressure;
- baseline and cap-height relationships;
- punctuation and hanging alignment;
- image / type depth;
- local contrast;
- whitespace distribution;
- small spacing inconsistencies;
- repeated module behavior;
- visual noise.

Optical correction may depart from exact arithmetic when perception requires it. The deviation must strengthen the intended structure rather than conceal imprecision.

### Stage 12 — Test at real viewing conditions

Inspect:

- full-resolution composition;
- feed thumbnail;
- actual mobile width;
- blur / squint hierarchy;
- expected platform overlays;
- safe-area and crop variants;
- contact-sheet sequence;
- evidence inspectability;
- exact-copy fit;
- low-light or low-contrast conditions when relevant.

If the primary message or dominant relationship disappears at actual viewing size, the design is not solved.

### Stage 13 — Prepare design handoff

Return an executable design system, not screenshots and vague style descriptions.

Include:

- selected composition and rationale;
- thumbnail exploration sheet;
- rejected alternatives and reasons;
- page-class definitions;
- grid and geometry;
- exact-copy layout and line behavior;
- typography specification reference;
- image–type relationship;
- protected and quiet zones;
- crop, mask, and layer requirements;
- representative design comps;
- full-sequence contact sheet;
- target-width proof;
- variants and tolerances;
- unresolved production risk;
- decisions requiring Art Director or ECD authority.

## Chinese and mixed-script awareness

Editorial Design must account for the visual density and square rhythm of Chinese characters.

Do not treat Chinese copy as English text translated into different glyphs.

Check:

- headline line breaks preserve meaning and rhetorical rhythm;
- punctuation does not create weak line starts or endings;
- mixed Chinese / Latin / numerals have controlled baseline, width, weight, and spacing;
- long Chinese lines do not become impenetrable texture;
- body copy measure and leading support mobile reading;
- display type remains recognisable when cropped or occluded;
- simplified and traditional variants are separately tested when required.

Detailed type decisions belong to the Typography Skill, but page design must make room for their consequences.

## Evidence-led design

When the page uses screenshots, interfaces, documents, charts, comparisons, or source evidence:

- make the evidence large enough to inspect;
- keep critical labels and values visible;
- do not crop away context that changes meaning;
- distinguish evidence from decorative reconstruction;
- annotate only what the viewer needs to see;
- direct attention without covering the proof;
- preserve source, date, version, limitation, or simulation labels when material.

A design that makes evidence beautiful but unreadable fails its editorial purpose.

## Common non-professional shortcuts

Reject:

- choosing a template before understanding the page;
- using one centered composition for every page;
- treating all text tiers at similar scale;
- making microtype small merely to look editorial;
- adding cards and rounded boxes because hierarchy is unresolved;
- placing type only in a generic top safe zone;
- using random crop as a substitute for tension;
- turning dimensional subjects into flat stickers without conceptual reason;
- generating an attractive image before exact copy geometry;
- preserving an image by shrinking or rewriting copy;
- applying the same page class regardless of editorial function;
- relying on a numerical score as proof of design quality.

## Specificity test

Ask:

> If the title and hero image were replaced with unrelated content, would the design still work almost unchanged?

If yes, the result is probably a reusable template rather than a project-specific design system.

The solution should embody a relationship, mechanism, evidence structure, visual metaphor, or content rhythm that belongs to this project.

## Failure diagnosis

Classify the earliest failure:

- unclear page purpose → Editorial Department;
- weak or generic visual thesis → Visual Concept / Art Director;
- sequence repetition → Storyboard / Editorial Design;
- hierarchy or geometry failure → Editorial Design;
- typeface, line break, spacing, or mixed-script failure → Typography;
- image cannot satisfy layout → Image Direction / image production;
- evidence cannot be read → Information Design / Editorial Design;
- production diverged from design → Production Department;
- target surface was missing → Creative Producer / Deliverable Contract.

Do not fix an upstream design problem with downstream decoration.

## Specialist self-check

Before returning to Art Director, inspect actual artifacts and answer:

- Are there materially different structural explorations?
- Is the selected composition justified by the communication problem?
- Does exact copy fit without semantic or hierarchy damage?
- Does the grid create coherent relationships?
- Do image and type form one system?
- Is the design specific to the project?
- Does every page have a distinct editorial and visual job?
- Does the full sequence have rhythm and progression?
- Does the design work at actual mobile width and thumbnail scale?
- Can Production execute without inventing missing design decisions?
