# Design Critique Skill

## Professional role

Design Critique is an independent visual review capability inside the Visual Department.

Its purpose is not to praise work, assign a decorative score, or repeat a checklist. It reconstructs the design's intended communication, inspects the actual artifact under real viewing conditions, identifies the earliest professional object where a defect became true, and recommends the smallest consequential rework.

The critic does not redesign the work during critique and does not grant Department clearance. It reports findings to Art Director.

## Independence rule

The person or model instance that produced the primary design should not be the sole critic of that design.

Compact work may combine execution seats, but Art Director must still obtain a separate critique pass before Department clearance.

## Required inputs

Require:

- Greenlit Treatment and ECD-aligned Script;
- exact copy and Frame Scripts;
- visual problem and selected visual thesis;
- target surface and viewing conditions;
- concept-route evidence;
- full-sequence contact sheet when multi-page;
- representative design comps;
- typography and image-direction references;
- evidence or source requirements;
- known limitations and open variables;
- stated design rationale.

Critique must inspect the actual artifact, not only a description of it.

## Critique sequence

### Step 1 — Reconstruct intent

State the design's intended effect in plain language:

```markdown
What should be noticed first:
What should be understood next:
What should remain after viewing:
What visual relationship is supposed to carry the idea:
What is fixed by upstream authority:
What is still open to design judgment:
```

Do not critique against a personal preference unrelated to the accepted project.

### Step 2 — Observe before interpreting

Record direct observations separately from conclusions.

Examples of observation:

- title occupies the upper-left quarter;
- three elements have similar visual weight;
- body copy crosses a high-detail image area;
- page 3 and page 4 use the same composition;
- evidence is unreadable at mobile width;
- the hero's face is covered by two text lines.

Examples of interpretation:

- hierarchy is ambiguous;
- the sequence stalls;
- image and type do not form one system;
- the design feels generic;
- the concept is not perceptible.

Observation supports diagnosis. Vague adjectives do not.

### Step 3 — Test the first glance

At feed-thumbnail size and with a blur / squint test, ask:

- What is the dominant mass?
- Is it the intended one?
- Are there competing visual centers?
- Is the primary statement recognisable?
- Does the image communicate the correct object, action, evidence, or relationship?
- Does the page promise what the sequence actually delivers?

A page that works only after close reading may be valid for some contexts, but that must be a deliberate contract decision.

### Step 4 — Test editorial hierarchy

Check whether perceptual order matches editorial priority.

Inspect:

- headline versus support;
- statement versus evidence;
- primary content versus metadata;
- source / limitation visibility;
- page markers and navigation;
- repeated phrases at different sizes;
- whether one page carries too many primary messages.

Hierarchy is not solved merely because font sizes differ.

### Step 5 — Test composition

Evaluate:

- balance and center of gravity;
- scale contrast;
- tension and stability;
- directional movement;
- edge relationships;
- crop pressure;
- figure / ground;
- alignment and deliberate misalignment;
- use of negative space;
- density distribution;
- whether the page has one governing structure.

Identify whether the composition is mathematically organized but optically weak, or apparently dynamic but structurally arbitrary.

### Step 6 — Test typography

Inspect actual language and rendered type:

- typeface fitness for role;
- hierarchy and voice;
- headline shape and line breaks;
- Chinese punctuation and mixed-script behavior;
- tracking, leading, measure, and rag;
- optical alignment;
- title recognition under crop or occlusion;
- body and evidence readability at target width;
- glyph, fallback, and exact-copy errors.

Do not call typography professional because it uses an expensive-looking font or small editorial microtype.

### Step 7 — Test image and type integration

Ask:

- Did exact copy influence image composition?
- Is the image–type relationship deliberate?
- Does overlap create depth or merely obstruct?
- Is quiet space designed or accidental?
- Does the crop strengthen the message?
- Does the subject retain volume, material, and spatial presence?
- Are masks, shadows, and local contrast plausible?
- Could the image be replaced without changing the layout?
- Could the layout be reused with unrelated content?

When image and type behave as two independent templates, identify which upstream decision failed: page design, image direction, asset production, or final assembly.

### Step 8 — Test project specificity

Apply the replacement test:

> If the title and hero were replaced with unrelated content, would the composition remain almost equally valid?

If yes, explain what project-specific relationship, mechanism, evidence structure, metaphor, or visual rhythm is absent.

Do not demand novelty for its own sake. Specificity means the design embodies this communication problem.

### Step 9 — Test sequence

For multi-page work, inspect the complete contact sheet and swipe order.

Check:

- opening promise;
- page-to-page progression;
- redundant pages;
- density and energy curve;
- repeated page templates;
- changes in image / type balance;
- evidence timing;
- continuity of objects, spaces, people, process, or system when relevant;
- climax, payoff, and ending;
- whether the final page completes or merely stops.

A set of good isolated pages can still be a weak sequence.

### Step 10 — Test evidence and information design

When the work contains proof, screenshots, data, interfaces, documents, comparisons, or diagrams:

- can the evidence be inspected at target size?
- are critical labels and values visible?
- do annotations point to the correct feature?
- has crop or simplification altered meaning?
- are simulation, reconstruction, speculation, or concept art correctly distinguished?
- do decorative elements imply unsupported facts?
- is the source or limitation visible where interpretation depends on it?

### Step 11 — Test target-surface behavior

Inspect:

- actual mobile width;
- feed thumbnail;
- platform crop;
- UI overlays;
- safe areas;
- language and aspect-ratio variants;
- low-quality compression where relevant;
- sequence as displayed by the platform.

A desktop-only review is not sufficient for mobile Social Editorial work.

### Step 12 — Locate the earliest failure

Classify the defect by professional object:

- project premise or governing logic;
- content architecture;
- Frame Script or exact copy;
- visual concept;
- sequence staging;
- editorial design;
- typography;
- image direction;
- information design;
- image production or retouch;
- finished art or export;
- Deliverable Contract or platform assumption.

The visible defect may appear late while originating upstream.

### Step 13 — Prioritize rework

Rank findings by consequence:

#### Blocking

The work communicates the wrong idea, lacks necessary content or evidence, cannot be read at target conditions, or violates authority / rights / factual constraints.

#### Structural

The concept, hierarchy, composition, sequence, typography system, or image–type relationship is not professionally resolved.

#### Craft

The governing design is sound but optical alignment, spacing, crop, masks, retouch, or consistency requires refinement.

#### Optional refinement

A change may improve nuance but is not necessary for the accepted communication and quality standard.

Do not bury a structural failure among minor polish notes.

## Critique language

Use specific, causal language.

Weak:

- make it more premium;
- improve hierarchy;
- the page feels empty;
- the image is not strong enough.

Actionable:

- the headline and hero have equal mass, so the first glance splits; reduce the hero crop or increase the title's structural dominance rather than adding decoration;
- page 4 repeats page 3's centered card layout despite a different editorial job; redesign page 4 as evidence-led and enlarge the screenshot until the claimed feature is inspectable;
- the final two Chinese characters form a weak third line and detach the verb from its object; test a two-line break or revise the column width before changing font size;
- the generated background places high-detail windows beneath support copy, forcing an opaque repair box; regenerate or extend the image from the approved layout constraints.

## No score-only approval

Scores may help compare revisions, but no numeric threshold can establish professional quality.

Every critical judgment must identify:

- observed evidence;
- communication consequence;
- earliest failed object;
- recommended rework;
- proof required to close the issue.

## Closure review

When a revision returns, the critic verifies the specific closure evidence rather than restarting with unrelated preferences.

For each finding, mark:

- Resolved;
- Partially Resolved;
- Not Resolved;
- Superseded by authorized change;
- Accepted limitation.

A revision that fixes the symptom while preserving the structural cause remains unresolved.

## Specialist return

The critique report returns to Art Director.

Art Director decides the department disposition, assigns rework, and remains responsible for visual sign-off. The critic does not communicate a formal decision directly to Creative Producer or the ECD.
