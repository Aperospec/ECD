# Bilingual Transcreation Skill

Constitution clause: `SEM-046`.

## Purpose

Bilingual or multilingual work is created from a shared semantic invariant. It is not one language written first and mechanically translated into another.

Each language version must be native to its locale, preserve the same consequential meaning, and expose its own layout implications.

This Skill returns to Editorial Director and does not perform final typography or layout.

## Inputs

- Greenlit Treatment;
- Content Architecture and Frame Scripts;
- shared source, claim, rights, evidence, and disclosure conditions;
- target languages and locales;
- audience relationship for each locale;
- terminology and proper-noun constraints;
- existing native drafts, when any;
- Editorial Director assignment.

## 1. Establish the shared semantic invariant

For every page, beat, and publication section record:

- editorial function;
- internal semantic proposition;
- subject and agency;
- action, relation, or claim;
- certainty and reality position;
- evidence, attribution, and limitation;
- emotional temperature;
- governing metaphor or conceptual vocabulary;
- required names, numbers, dates, versions, and units;
- intended audience change;
- CTA or final question, when material.

The invariant is the parity source. Neither language's first draft is automatically the master.

## 2. Build language-specific briefs

For each language and locale define:

- audience relationship and cultural context;
- person and point of view;
- formality and social distance;
- headline and sentence conventions;
- degree of compression;
- punctuation, capitalization, date, number, and quotation conventions;
- culturally natural examples or references;
- terminology, transliteration, capitalization, and untranslated terms;
- expected line-density and layout consequences.

## 3. Produce native drafts

Draft each version from the shared invariant and its language-specific brief.

Do not preserve source-language word order, clause structure, rhetorical device, sentence count, or line length when they produce unnatural target-language copy.

Preserve the function and consequence instead.

## 4. Apply native-language craft

Use the appropriate registered native-language craft Skill for every supported language version.

For Chinese and English in V3.1:

- `ecd-chinese-copy-craft`;
- `ecd-english-copy-craft`.

A bilingual pair is not ready merely because both versions are understandable. Each must independently pass native-language craft and Copy Editing.

## 5. Review functional and semantic parity

Compare versions across:

- page function;
- subject and agency;
- governing idea;
- promise and payoff;
- certainty and reality position;
- evidence and attribution;
- qualifications and limitations;
- emotional temperature;
- CTA or closing consequence;
- terminology consistency.

The versions do not need identical:

- word count;
- clause order;
- sentence count;
- punctuation;
- rhetorical device;
- title capitalization;
- line geometry.

## 6. Resolve transcreation conflicts

When direct translation sounds unnatural or changes emphasis:

1. return to the shared semantic invariant;
2. identify the source expression's function;
3. create a native target-language solution serving the same function;
4. record any material difference in tone, certainty, cultural implication, or evidence;
5. rerun native craft and parity review.

Do not sacrifice subject, agency, qualification, evidence, or limitation merely to match length.

## 7. Hand layout consequences to Visual

For each language record:

- probable headline shape and line count;
- density and expansion risk;
- unbreakable names, numbers, or terminology;
- mixed-script behavior;
- culturally required punctuation or capitalization;
- content that may move between headline, deck, and support without semantic loss;
- variant-specific copy footprint.

These are Editorial constraints. Final line breaks, typography, and page geometry remain Visual responsibilities after Script Alignment.

## Self-check

- Does each version read as native writing rather than a calque?
- Are page function, agency, certainty, evidence, and limitations equivalent?
- Are differences in syntax, rhetoric, and length deliberate?
- Is terminology consistent and appropriate to each locale?
- Did each version pass its native-language craft review?
- Are layout consequences explicit without pre-empting Visual design?
- Does any cultural adaptation change an ECD-approved meaning or claim?

## Failure routing

- shared meaning is unstable → Content Architecture / Development;
- source or claim differs by locale → Research Verification / Claims Rights;
- one version is unnatural → relevant native-language craft Skill;
- material parity conflict changes the approved Script → Editorial Director / Producer for renewed alignment;
- layout cannot support language variants without semantic loss → Visual consultation through Producer.
