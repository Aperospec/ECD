# Visual Development Reference Canon

## Purpose

This canon supplies professional method sources for ECD's internal Art Director and Visual Development function.

Books, academic methods, studio practices, and standards are treated as **compressed craft systems**. The studio extracts operational principles, converts them into original procedures and tests, and applies them to the current project. It does not reproduce source text, copy examples, or imitate a named artist's recognizable style.

Use this canon through:

- `visual-problem-framing.md`;
- `visual-intent-elicitation.md`;
- `reference-reading-and-transfer.md`;
- `visual-metaphor-mapping.md`;
- `visual-concept-development.md`;
- `anchor-keyframe-gate.md`;
- `ecd-friendly-visual-alignment.md`;
- `visual-critique-and-failure-diagnosis.md`.

## Priority Order

When methods or references conflict, prioritize:

1. fidelity to the Greenlit Creative Treatment;
2. fidelity to the Accepted Creative Script and Written Visual Beats;
3. the ECD's source instruction and authority class;
4. semantic clarity and audience perception;
5. world, sequence, and formal coherence;
6. originality and sufficient distance from references;
7. production feasibility;
8. surface polish.

A beautiful image that solves the wrong visual problem fails.

## Design Framing

### Kees Dorst — *Frame Innovation* and design-framing practice

Operational contribution:

- distinguish the user's underlying value from the first literal solution;
- identify when the studio has silently reformulated the goal;
- create alternative frames that preserve the accepted value;
- test whether a proposed visual solution still addresses the original project.

Use for:

- Visual Problem Statements;
- detecting goal-reformulation failure;
- generating materially different concept routes;
- deciding whether a new visual metaphor belongs to the project or changes it.

## Visual Meaning and Context

### Gunther Kress and Theo van Leeuwen — *Reading Images*

Operational contribution:

- analyze representational content, viewer relation, salience, framing, and composition;
- understand how an image organizes meaning rather than merely which objects it contains;
- distinguish the depicted subject from the visual grammar used to make it dominant, distant, intimate, authoritative, or exploratory.

Use for:

- Reference Reading Records;
- salience and viewer-position analysis;
- image–type relationship;
- testing what the audience perceives first, second, and third.

### Gillian Rose — *Visual Methodologies*

Operational contribution:

- distinguish the image itself, its production conditions, its circulation context, and its audience interpretation;
- avoid treating a successful reference image as a context-free style token;
- ask why a visual works in its original setting and whether that effect transfers to the present project.

Use for:

- reference context and transfer limits;
- platform and audience interpretation;
- distinguishing concept reference from content, style, or composition reference.

## Formal Visual Structure

### Bruce Block — *The Visual Story*

Operational contribution:

- treat space, line, shape, tone, color, movement, and rhythm as controllable variables;
- build contrast and affinity intentionally across a sequence;
- use formal progression to support editorial progression rather than repeating one palette and one composition.

Use for:

- thumbnail and value studies;
- sequence differentiation;
- color scripts;
- formal continuity and controlled contrast.

### Marcos Mateu-Mestre — *Framed Ink*

Operational contribution:

- use large shapes, value grouping, visual weight, line of action, and staging to control attention;
- solve narrative composition before detail and rendering;
- diagnose when atmosphere is hiding a weak composition.

Use for:

- composition thumbnails;
- black-and-white studies;
- focal hierarchy;
- subject–environment power relationships.

## Concept and World Development

### *The Skillful Huntsman*

Operational contribution:

- move from research through sketches, multiple directions, environmental logic, and keyframes;
- show process evidence rather than only polishing the first idea;
- develop character, architecture, environment, and story as one system.

Use for:

- concept-route development;
- world-logic boards;
- low-cost exploration before anchor production;
- keyframe selection.

### Scott Robertson and Thomas Bertling — *How to Draw*

Operational contribution:

- construct believable spatial systems from perspective, proportion, primitive forms, and design logic;
- design nonexistent environments that remain physically and spatially credible;
- separate spatial construction from surface decoration.

Use for:

- world topology;
- architectural scale;
- camera and perspective feasibility;
- environment continuity between establishing and body frames.

## Color and Light

### James Gurney — *Color and Light*

Operational contribution:

- reason from light sources, material response, atmospheric depth, local color, reflected light, and color relationships;
- make speculative worlds visually credible rather than relying on fashionable grading;
- use color and light to distinguish sequence states without breaking the shared world.

Use for:

- style boards;
- anchor keyframes;
- color scripts;
- material and atmospheric consistency.

## Elicitation and Co-Design

### Visual Thinking Strategies

Operational contribution:

- begin from visible evidence;
- paraphrase the participant's observation accurately;
- connect observations into a shared interpretation;
- ask what in the image supports the judgment rather than demanding specialist vocabulary.

Use for:

- visual-intent elicitation;
- reference discussion with a non-specialist ECD;
- turning “this feels right” into testable hypotheses.

### Repertory-grid and triadic comparison methods

Operational contribution:

- discover the ECD's own evaluation dimensions by comparing alternatives;
- avoid imposing the Art Director's adjective list;
- expose meaningful contrasts such as “complete world versus collection of attractions” or “world-led versus character-led.”

Use for:

- ECD-friendly route comparison;
- distinguishing mood, structure, style, and content preferences;
- resolving ambiguous reference intent.

### Evaluation-grid / laddering methods

Operational contribution:

- move upward from a visual attribute to the value it serves;
- move downward from an abstract preference to observable visual evidence;
- connect feature, perception, and project consequence.

Use for:

- translating “alive,” “grand,” “warm,” “too commercial,” or “ugly” into operational design criteria.

## Art Criticism and Formal Analysis

Use the professional sequence:

```text
Description
→ Formal Analysis
→ Interpretation
→ Evaluation / Transfer
```

Operational contribution:

- separate what is visible from what is inferred;
- prevent reference interpretation from becoming unexamined imitation;
- diagnose where a visual failed: problem frame, metaphor, world, composition, affect, sequence, or execution.

Use for:

- Reference Reading Records;
- visual critique;
- first-failed-object routing.

## Studio Visual Development Practice

Generalized animation, film, game, and concept-design practice contributes:

- research before design;
- multiple concept routes;
- mood, style, concept, and world-logic boards as separate objects;
- thumbnails and value studies before finish;
- keyframes to prove world and story;
- color scripts to control sequence progression;
- representative proof before broad production.

Do not use “studio practice” as permission to copy a studio's visual identity or a known production's scene language.

## Canon Use Rule

For each project, Art Director should select only the methods that resolve the current visual uncertainty.

Do not display a bibliography to the ECD unless useful. The visible result should be a clearer visual problem, stronger alternatives, better evidence, and a decision the ECD can make without becoming an art specialist.
